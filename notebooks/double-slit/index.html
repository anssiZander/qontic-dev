One electron, two slits

The double-slit experiment in seven short parts. Every figure on this page uses the same wave model as the Q-Ontic analytical double-slit app, with the same default settings, so what you see here is what you will see there.

Electrons are sent one at a time toward a wall with two narrow openings. Behind the wall is a detector screen. Each electron makes one small spot. After many electrons, the spots form bright and dark bands.

Everyone agrees on that pattern, and on how to calculate it. The disagreement is about what is going on between the source and the screen. This notebook first builds the calculation, then shows three accounts of what it describes: Orthodox, Pilot-Wave and Many-Worlds.

1The puzzle

The figure shows the experiment seen from above, with the same layout as the app. The source is on the left, the wall with two slits in the middle, the detector screen on the right. The blue shading is the wavefunction, moving from left to right. Brightness shows |ψ|², rescaled at each distance from the source so the pattern stays visible as the wave spreads. The moving stripes are the crests of the wave, one wavelength apart. The curve to the right of the screen is the probability of a hit at each height y.

Start with only the upper slit open. Electrons arriving at the screen spread into a single broad hump. Switch to the lower slit: another hump, shifted down. Now open both.

Predict first

If each electron goes through one slit or the other, what should the screen show with both slits open? Sketch it, then press Add the two humps to see that prediction, and Both slits to see what happens.

The two-slit pattern is not the sum of the two one-slit patterns. Some places that each slit alone would reach are nearly empty when both are open. Opening a second slit made it less likely for an electron to arrive there.

The spacing of the bands depends on the wavelength λ and the slit separation d. For a screen at distance Z behind the wall, the bright bands are separated by roughly

\[ \Delta y \approx \frac{\lambda Z}{d}. \]

Move the sliders and check this against the readout. A longer wavelength spreads the bands apart. Moving the slits apart squeezes them together.

Where does λ come from for an electron?

De Broglie's relation λ = h/p assigns a wavelength to any particle with momentum p. The app's default, λ = 50 nm, corresponds to a very slow electron. The app keeps all lengths in nanometers so that the source, the slits and the screen fit on one picture. Real electron experiments use other scales, but the pattern has the same structure. Bach and coworkers (2013) carried out the full version with electrons, opening and closing each slit with a movable mask (Part 7).

In the app

  1. Open the app in Orthodox view and press Start. Watch the hits build up.
  2. Press Both Slits to cycle to one slit, then the other. Compare the histograms.
  3. In the Physics tab, change λ and the slit separation. Does Δy ≈ λZ/d hold?

2One electron, one dot

The smooth curve in Part 1 is never seen directly. What the screen records are single events. Each electron produces one localized spot, at one place. In the figure the hits appear on the screen itself, and the histogram beside it counts them.

Predict first

Press Send one. Will the screen show one spot, two spots (one per slit), or a faint copy of the whole pattern?

hits on the screen, and counted per detector pixel predicted counts from |ψ|²

One run gives one dot. Where that dot lands cannot be predicted. Only the frequencies can: after many electrons, the histogram follows the curve. The rule connecting the two is the Born rule. The probability of a hit near position y on the screen is proportional to |ψ(y)|², the squared magnitude of the wavefunction there.

Each electron is sent alone. The pattern is not produced by electrons interacting with each other. It is a property of the setup, revealed one electron at a time. This was first shown cleanly for electrons by Tonomura and coworkers in 1989.

3Add, then square

With both slits open, the wavefunction at the screen is a sum of two parts: one coming from the upper opening and one from the lower opening.

\[ \psi = \psi_U + \psi_L \]

In the figure, the colored lines run from each slit to a point P on the screen. Each part of ψ at P is a complex number: it has a size and a direction, drawn on the right as an arrow. The rule is to add the arrows first and square the result afterwards.

\[ |\psi_U+\psi_L|^2 \;=\; |\psi_U|^2 + |\psi_L|^2 \;+\; \underbrace{2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(\psi_U^{*}\psi_L\right)}_{\text{interference term}} \]

The first two terms are the two humps from Part 1. The last term can be positive or negative. It is what makes bright and dark bands.

Try it

Drag P along the screen, or use the slider, until the two arrows point in opposite directions. What happens to the probability there?

ψU, from the upper slit ψL, from the lower slit sum ψU + ψL probability, both slits

At a dark band the two arrows have about the same length and point in opposite directions. They cancel. The two alternatives are not two electrons colliding: they are two terms in the description of a single electron.

Why do the arrows turn as P moves? The two lines to P have different lengths, rU and rL. Every extra wavelength of path turns an arrow once around. Bright bands sit where rL − rU is a whole number of wavelengths, dark bands halfway between. Check this in the readout. The path difference grows by λ for every step of about λZ/d along the screen, which gives the band spacing.

4Which slit?

Suppose we place a detector at the upper slit that records whether the electron passed there. The record can be anything physical: a click, a flipped spin, a changed atom. Nobody needs to look at it.

Predict first

With a reliable record at the upper slit, what happens to the bands on the screen? Move the slider from No record to Reliable record and watch the wave behind the wall as well.

screen pattern with this record no record (full bands) reliable record (two humps added)

A record correlates the electron with the detector. The full state is then

\[ \psi_U\,|\text{record: upper}\rangle \;+\; \psi_L\,|\text{record: lower}\rangle . \]

The interference term gets multiplied by the overlap γ between the two possible detector states. If the records are perfectly distinguishable, γ = 0 and the bands disappear: the screen shows the two humps added. If the records cannot be told apart, γ = 1 and the bands are complete. In between, the bands fade.

The app's which-slit detector is an ideal one: it always corresponds to γ = 0. The slider here lets you see the in-between cases.

In the app

  1. Press No Detector to cycle to a detector at one slit. The two components are drawn in cyan and orange.
  2. Run until the histogram is clear. Then switch the detector off and compare.

5Same pattern, three accounts

Everything so far is shared. All three accounts below use the same wavefunction and predict the same screen statistics. They disagree about what exists and what happens between the source and the screen. The app has one view for each.

View 1

Orthodox

The wavefunction is the complete description of the electron, and it gives probabilities for measurement results. No path is assigned between source and screen. At detection, one result occurs and the wavefunction is replaced by one that matches it. This step, often called collapse, is not described by the Schrödinger equation.

In the app: no trajectories are drawn. The packet fades as hits are registered.

Open in Orthodox view
View 2

Pilot-Wave

The electron is a particle with a definite position at all times. The wavefunction is also real, and it guides the particle. The particle goes through one slit. The wave goes through both, and the particle's motion depends on both parts. The randomness comes from not knowing the exact starting position.

In the app: each sampled particle is drawn with its trajectory.

Open in Pilot-Wave view
View 3

Many-Worlds

The wavefunction is everything, and it never collapses. When the electron reaches the screen, the screen and the electron become correlated: each possible hit position goes with a detector showing that hit. Each of these branches is equally real. An observer in one branch sees one hit.

In the app: turn on Slow-motion branching to watch the split.

Open in Many-Worlds view

Pilot-wave trajectories, computed here

Trajectories are the one picture that can be drawn only for the Pilot-Wave account, so this figure computes them directly. Each particle starts at a random position in the source beam and follows the guidance equation, using the same wavefunction as Parts 1–4. Particles that hit the wall are absorbed and not shown. Cyan particles went through the upper slit, orange through the lower.

Look at the histogram on the right. Without a detector, every cyan particle lands on the upper half of the screen and every orange one on the lower half. The trajectories never cross the center line. This follows from the guidance equation and the symmetry of the setup. Turn on the detector and the rule breaks: each particle is now guided by one component alone.

The guidance equation used here

The particle moves forward at the packet speed. Its sideways motion follows the phase of the wavefunction:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{1}{k}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\frac{\partial_y\psi}{\psi}\right), \qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}. \]

This is the same law the app uses. It is integrated here with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step of about 2 nm. The first published pictures of double-slit trajectories of this kind are in Philippidis, Dewdney and Hiley (1979). For photons, Kocsis and coworkers (2011) reconstructed average trajectories with weak measurements; they show the same pattern of paths that bunch into the bright bands without crossing the center line.

7About the model, and going further

The figures here and in the app use the same simplified model. The beam moves forward at constant speed and spreads only sideways, a standard approximation for narrow, nearly straight beams. The slits are smooth openings that absorb what they do not let through, rather than a detailed wall. Defaults: λ = 50 nm, slit separation 300 nm, slit width σ = 30 nm, source width σ = 200 nm, source-to-wall 225 nm, wall-to-screen Z = 1000 nm. The app's Physics tab gives the full equations and their limits.
